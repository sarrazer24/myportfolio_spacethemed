At some point, being a Marvel fan started requiring a second lifetime. Between the timelines, the multiverses, the post-credit scenes, and the ever-growing list of things to catch up on, I sometimes wonder whether keeping up with the MCU should come with a degree of its own.

I’ve been a Marvel fan for as long as I can remember. These characters and their stories have accompanied me through so many different stages of my life. But somewhere along the way, life happened. University, projects, deadlines, and the beautiful chaos of trying to figure everything out took over, and I gradually fell behind.

Not that I ever stopped loving Marvel. I simply needed a little more time in my own universe.

Eventually, I found my way back, and this time, it was VisionQuest.

And perhaps I couldn’t have picked a more interesting story to return to.

There’s something almost ironic about an artificial being searching for his humanity. A synthetic being, a creation that was never supposed to experience the world quite as we do, finding himself entangled in the very things that make us human: love, loss, memory, grief, and the endless search for identity.

Yet, somehow, he manages to embody some of the most profoundly human experiences we struggle to put into words.

His journey is one of losing himself, confronting the ghosts of his past, and eventually realizing that he cannot separate who he was from who he has become. That every version of himself, every memory, every experience, every possibility, belongs to the same story.

But among all the beautiful writing and philosophical questions the show explores, there is one particular line that made me stop and simply admire the words.

A line so magnificently written that I might just memorize it to the grave:

> “You have put me on a pedestal so impossibly high, and not to honor me, not to honor me, but to spare yourself the effort of the climb.”

And isn’t that something?

## Why do we put people on pedestals?
There is something profoundly human about the way we look up to others.

We see their accomplishments, their brilliance, their discipline, their success, and somewhere along the way, we begin to imagine them as something greater than ourselves. We place them on pedestals, sometimes so high that we can no longer see them for who they truly are.

We see the beauty of their achievements but rarely the bruises beneath them. We see the summit but not the mountains they had to cross. We admire the flowers without ever wondering how many winters their gardens endured.

And perhaps, sometimes, we do this not to honor them, but to spare ourselves the effort of the climb.

Because isn’t it easier to admire something from afar than to attempt to reach it? Isn’t it easier to convince ourselves that someone is simply extraordinary, that they possess something we could never have, than to accept that perhaps the only thing separating us from where they stand is the work we haven’t yet done?

We turn people into unreachable ideals. We convince ourselves that their achievements belong to another realm, another kind of person, another kind of life.

And somewhere along the way, admiration becomes an excuse.

We compare our beginnings to someone else’s chapters, our unfinished stories to their beautifully written endings. We look at the flowers in someone else’s garden and forget that ours, too, could bloom if only we tended to them.

We see the polished version and forget the process. We see the light and overlook the countless nights it took to find it.

We put people on pedestals so high that we no longer see them as people. And in doing so, we make ourselves smaller.

## Are we seeing the world, or merely looking through rose-colored glasses?
Perhaps we do something similar with the way we perceive life itself.

We glamorize things. We romanticize success, intelligence, talent, and achievement. We paint beautiful pictures of what life should look like, of who we should become, of where we ought to be by a certain age.

We look through rose-colored glasses and fall in love with the picture they create.

And sometimes, we’re so busy looking through that window that we forget to look in the mirror.

We spend so much time looking outward, admiring what others have accomplished, imagining how extraordinary their lives must be, and wondering how they managed to become everything we wish we were, that we forget to turn that gaze inward.

We forget to look at the person standing right in front of us.

The person with their own dreams, their own quiet ambitions, their own untapped potential. The person who, perhaps, has been waiting for a little faith, a little patience, and the courage to begin.

We forget that the people we admire probably started somewhere, too.

They had doubts. They stumbled. They failed. They questioned themselves. They had days when the road seemed endless and the summit nothing more than a distant silhouette against the sky.

We rarely see those moments. We arrive when the flowers have bloomed, without having witnessed the seasons of rain that came before them.

And perhaps the people we’ve been looking up to aren’t as fundamentally different from us as we’ve convinced ourselves.

Perhaps they simply kept going.

Perhaps they dared to try, dared to fail, dared to begin again when everything seemed to tell them otherwise.

And perhaps we are far more capable than we have ever allowed ourselves to believe.

## What if the mountain isn’t as impossible as we think?
The easy route has always been tempting. Giving up can be deceptively comfortable. There is a certain comfort in standing at the foot of a mountain and convincing ourselves that its summit was never meant for us.

But the easy route has never necessarily been the right one.

The right one is often difficult. It asks for patience when we have none left, courage when fear is louder, and faith when the destination is nowhere in sight.

But every step of the way is worth it.

Every tear, every drop of sweat, every setback, every sleepless night, every moment when we question ourselves. Every little piece of ourselves that we pour into something we genuinely want.

You might not realize it while you’re climbing. You might lose your vision for a moment, lose your direction, or find yourself wondering whether all this trouble is worth anything at all.

There will be days when the summit seems to move further away with every step. Days when your feet ache, when your heart feels heavy, when the road is so steep that looking back seems easier than moving forward.

But perhaps it is precisely through the hardship, the grief, the pain, the suffering, and the patience that we begin to understand how precious the climb truly is.

You wouldn’t know just how dear something is without understanding what it took to reach it.

Whether it’s a small step or a mountain as high as Everest, the effort matters.

And you might not see it now, but one day, you will look back. You will see all the little steps that brought you here, all the moments you thought you couldn’t make it through, all the times you almost gave up but chose to try once more.

And perhaps you’ll realize that it was worth it.

Not because the road was beautiful every step of the way, but because you kept walking despite the days when it wasn’t.

## And what happens when we finally turn our gaze inward?
There’s another beautiful line from the show:

> “But what is grief, if not love persevering?”

It’s such a tragically poetic way of putting something so difficult into words.

Vision’s journey is, at its heart, about accepting his grief, his memories, and the different versions of himself he had spent so long trying to separate. He eventually realizes that he cannot simply leave behind the person he once was to make sense of the person he has become.

And I think there’s something worth reflecting on there.

Sometimes, we become so preoccupied with who we think we should be, or who we once were, that we fail to recognize who we actually are.

We spend so much time chasing a future version of ourselves that we forget to acknowledge the person doing all the work to get there.

We want the achievement without the uncertainty, the destination without the exhaustion, the beautiful ending without having to live through all the imperfect chapters that precede it.

But becoming someone is rarely a graceful or straightforward journey.

We are every version of ourselves. The child who dreamed without limits, the person who stumbled along the way, the one who succeeded, the one who failed, the one who gave up, and the one who found the courage to begin again.

We are our memories, our missed opportunities, our regrets, our victories, and all the possibilities that never came to fruition.

Perhaps finding ourselves isn’t about choosing which version is the real one, but learning to embrace them all.

And perhaps we should extend that same grace to the person we see in the mirror.

We spend so much time looking up that we forget to look within. We admire other people’s journeys while overlooking our own potential. We convince ourselves that the people we aspire to become are somehow beyond our reach, when perhaps all that separates us from them is the effort we have yet to put in.

So maybe it’s time we stopped looking at the world exclusively through rose-colored glasses and started looking at ourselves with a little more honesty, a little more compassion, and a little more faith.

Perhaps the person we’ve been waiting to become has been waiting for us to take that first step all along.

Stop admiring the summit from a distance.

Stop convincing yourself that the climb is reserved for someone else.

Look in the mirror.

And if we spend our lives admiring mountains from afar, convincing ourselves that their summits belong to someone else, only to discover one day that we were capable of climbing them all along, then how many mountains will we have left untraveled simply because we never believed we could?
